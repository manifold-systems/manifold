\title{Interface-Scoped Dispatch:\texorpdfstring{\\}{ }General Delegation with \texorpdfstring{$\boldsymbol{O(1)}$}{O(1)} Self-Calls}

\author{R. Scott McKinney}
\affiliation{\institution{Manifold Systems}\country{USA}}
\email{scott@manifold.systems}
\renewcommand{\shortauthors}{McKinney}

\begin{abstract}
The conceptual appeal of object composition lies in its ability to build loosely coupled systems from independent runtime
components. Yet conventional object composition lacks late-bound self-calls, or \emph{open recursion}, a defining strength
of implementation inheritance. Restoring open recursion while retaining the properties of object composition remains an
unmet challenge.

We present \emph{Parts}, a general delegation model that unifies independent runtime components, arbitrary composition,
and open recursion. Its enabling mechanism, interface-scoped dispatch (ISD), assigns composite identity per interface at
construction, allowing self-calls to dynamically dispatch through overriding composites. Parts is realized as a compiler
plugin for Java 8--27 with IDE integration. It achieves $O(1)$ dispatch cost that is empirically equivalent to a standard
vtable call, providing open recursion through composition without sacrificing the independence and flexibility that make
object composition attractive.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Object composition is the standard approach to structuring behavior from reusable components~\cite{gamma1994}. Most
object-oriented languages support composition through some form of \emph{forwarding}~\cite{kniesel2000}, providing an
alternative to implementation inheritance and its well-known hazards, including fragile base classes and coupling to
superclass internals. Forwarding, however, gives up a property that makes inheritance particularly useful:
\emph{open recursion}. With open recursion, a component's self-calls remain late-bound, allowing them to reach
overrides supplied by a surrounding composition. Without it, self-calls are fixed to the component's local
implementation and cannot reach such overrides. Thus, conventional composition preserves independent components but
loses the dynamic self-dispatch that makes inheritance effective for extensible behavior.

The challenge is to recover open recursion without giving up the essential properties of object composition. A model
that fully meets this challenge must provide three properties: \emph{independent components}, meaning late-bound objects
supplied at construction; \emph{arbitrary composition}, meaning unrestricted composition topologies (Section~\ref{subsec:topologies}); and
\emph{open recursion}, meaning self-calls that remain late-bound through the composite. Existing composition
approaches achieve at most two of these three properties (Section~\ref{sec:related}).

The composition mechanisms of conventional languages differ in syntax and implementation but reduce to forwarding.
Some, such as Rust and Swift, require forwarding to be written explicitly; others, including Kotlin's \code{by}
clause~\cite{kotlin} and Go's struct embedding~\cite{go}, provide it automatically. These mechanisms support independent
components under arbitrary composition, but forwarding alone cannot preserve the composite identity required for open
recursion: a component's \code{this} remains the component itself, so its self-calls cannot be redirected through a
composite. This is the \emph{Object Schizophrenia} problem~\cite{sekharaiah2002,kniesel2000}.

Trait composition, including Scala's \code{with}~\cite{odersky2005} and mixins~\cite{bracha1990}, makes the opposite
trade. Self-calls through \code{this} dispatch within the composed hierarchy, so overrides remain visible. But traits
and mixins are fused into the hierarchy at compile time and execute as a single runtime object. The constituent
components therefore lose independent runtime identity, while composition is statically fixed rather than dynamically
defined.

Several research projects (Section~\ref{sec:related}) have explored \emph{delegation}~\cite{lieberman1986,kniesel1999}
as a means of retaining independent components while restoring open recursion. These models improve on conventional
forwarding, but so far support only restricted composition topologies rather than the arbitrary topologies available
through ordinary forwarding (Section~\ref{subsec:topologies}). Many also introduce auxiliary mechanisms to maintain
composite identity, which increase implementation complexity and, in some cases, incur substantial runtime overhead.

We present \emph{Parts}, a general delegation model that resolves these long-standing limitations by combining independent runtime
components, arbitrary composition, and open recursion. Its enabling mechanism, \emph{interface-scoped dispatch} (ISD), uses \emph{links} to assign
composite identity per interface at construction to \emph{parts}, components that participate in delegation. A part's
self-calls then dispatch dynamically through the composites that claim the corresponding interfaces. Because identity
is assigned per interface rather than per component, different interfaces of the same component may participate in
different composites, allowing open recursion under arbitrary composition topologies while preserving independent
runtime objects. Links also support non-part components, providing ordinary forwarding when delegation is not required,
so delegation and forwarding can coexist within the same composite.

The model's guarantees are established constructively: the dispatch invariant and $O(1)$ self-dispatch cost follow by construction,
and the safety rules by static enforcement in the compiler and IDE (Section~\ref{sec:impl}). A mechanized metatheory is
a natural complement left to future work (Section~\ref{sec:futurework}).

The core contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Parts}: a general delegation model in which independent runtime
        components can be composed arbitrarily, with partial delegation,
        open-recursive self-dispatch, and statically enforced interface contracts.
        The model is defined over standard interface hierarchies with a central
        dispatch invariant (Invariant~\ref{prop:dispatch}) and composite-scoped
        encapsulation (\code{@internal}).

  \item \textbf{Interface-scoped dispatch (ISD)}: the enabling mechanism that
        assigns composite identity per interface at construction, allowing
        open recursion under arbitrary composition through partial delegation,
        where a composite may claim a proper subset of a part's interfaces,
        with self-calls resolved in $O(1)$ time.

  \item \textbf{An implementation}: an open-source compiler plugin~\cite{mckinney2026}
        for Java 8--27 that realizes Parts with $O(1)$ self-dispatch at vtable speed,
        together with an IntelliJ IDEA plugin.
\end{itemize}

\section{Overview}
%% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Consider a minimal example that shows \code{@part} and \code{@link} working in a concrete single-rooted composite.

\begin{lstlisting}
interface Actor {
  void takeAction();
  void attack();
}

@part class Hero implements Actor {
  public void takeAction() { attack(); }
  public void attack() { println("Swing club!"); }
}

class Wizard implements Actor {
  @link Actor actor;
  Wizard(Actor actor) { this.actor = actor; }
  public void attack() { println("Cast spell!"); }
}

new Wizard(new Hero()).takeAction(); // "Cast spell!"
\end{lstlisting}

When \code{Wizard} is constructed, the compiler wires \code{Hero}'s
composite identity for \code{Actor} to \code{Wizard}. A call to
\code{takeAction()} dispatches to \code{Hero.takeAction()}, and the self-call
\code{attack()} within it dispatches back through \code{Wizard}, invoking
\code{Wizard.attack()}, not \code{Hero.attack()}.

This is the decorator pattern with one addition: the wrapped object's self-call
routes back through the composite. A plain decorator would forward
\code{takeAction()} to the \code{Hero}, where the \code{attack()} call would stay on
\code{Hero} (``Swing club!''), losing the override.

Because the link field accepts any \code{Actor} at construction
time, the part is a real late-bound object, not fused into the class
hierarchy. A different \code{Actor} implementation can be substituted
without changing \code{Wizard}.

Interface contracts are enforced at compile time. The link field has
type \code{Actor}, and the compiler verifies that \code{Wizard}
implements the delegated interface. A raw part may not escape the
composite dispatch path: passing or assigning \code{this} as the concrete
part type is a compile error; interface types are required (Section~\ref{subsec:safety}).

This example is single-rooted where \code{Wizard} is the sole composite for \code{Hero}, and one composite identity suffices.
The next section shows where a single composite identity breaks down, and how assigning identity per interface fixes it.

\subsection{Motivating example}

The following example shows a part with interfaces rooted at different
composites. \code{Hero} implements both \code{Combatant} and \code{Actor},
and two wrappers decorate it. A \code{Wizard} changes how it fights
(\code{Combatant}), while a \code{Demon} partially possesses it, seizing its name
and will (\code{Actor}) but leaving its trained combat untouched. As in the
overview, the wrapped hero's self-calls route back through the wrappers; now two
wrappers claim different interfaces of the same part. So from a single \code{Hero}
instance, \code{attack()} must dispatch to \code{Wizard} and \code{name()}
to \code{Demon}. A single composite identity cannot serve both. Assigning a
separate composite identity per interface resolves this.

\subsection{Definitions}

Parts is defined over a standard typed object model where classes implement interfaces and method dispatch is defined by
the receiver's runtime type.

\begin{description}
  \item[claim] an interface of a part is claimed when a composite links that interface, making the composite its exclusive
  identity within the part.

  \item[component] a class nominally implementing one or more interfaces, available for delegation if the class is a part
  and for forwarding otherwise.

  \item[composite] a class containing one or more links. A composite nominally implements the interfaces declared on its
  links.

  \item[delegation surface] a component's interface hierarchy, excluding any interface marked \code{@internal} in its \code{implements}
  clause.

  \item[interface] an abstract type that defines a contract (a Java interface).

  \item[link] an ordinary field annotated with \code{@link} and typed as an interface. The enclosing class (the composite)
  delegates the link's declared interface to the component assigned to the link at construction. Links are implicitly \code{private}
  and \code{final}; explicit modifiers are redundant.

  \item[part] a component annotated with \code{@part}, enabling compiler plugin support for open recursion.

  \item[partial delegation] a requirement for arbitrary compositions, where a composite may claim only some of a part's
  delegation surface.

  \item[root (of an interface)] the linking composite claiming an interface of a part.
\end{description}

When a composite links an interface to a part, the part is directly wired to the composite. It records a \emph{composite
identity}: a reference to the composite statically indexed by the interface. The part's self-calls route through the composite
indexed by the interface of the called method. The enabling mechanism is \emph{interface-scoped dispatch} (ISD).

A part may itself contain link fields, in which case composite identity propagates transitively. When such a part is wired
to an outer composite, that composite becomes the dispatch root for each interface it claims throughout the delegation chain,
while interfaces it does not claim retain the roots established by inner composites. This \emph{transitive propagation}
continues through a link field only when the field's value is a part and the field's declared interface and the claimed
interface are comparable, one a subtype of the other (Figure~\ref{fig:linkPartToSelf-listing}).

Composites must also resolve \emph{diamonds}. When a composite has multiple links that share a common interface, a sharing
link must be chosen explicitly via \code{@link(share=I.class)} at one of the link sites. This determines the leg of the diamond the
linking composite forwards through for its implementation of the interface. Diamonds consisting of multiple shared interfaces require the same \code{share=I.class}
to select the sharing links for each interface, where a single link can share multiple interfaces or the interfaces
can be shared separately from any of the other links capable of sharing them. Wiring is unaffected: self-calls on common
interfaces from other legs of the diamond naturally route through the shared leg.

Two practical extensions, composite-scoped encapsulation (\code{@internal}) and abstract parts with deferred linkage (\code{asLink()}),
are defined and implemented (Section~\ref{sec:impl}).

\paragraph{Dispatch rule.}
Traditional object-oriented dispatch resolves a call through the receiver's object identity:
\[ \text{call } m() \text{ on } o \;\longrightarrow\; \mathit{vtable}(o,\, m) \]
Interface-scoped dispatch replaces the single object identity with a per-interface composite identity. A self-call $m()$
in part $P$ where $m \in I$ resolves through the composite identity for $I$:
\[ \text{self-call } m() \in I \text{ in } P \;\longrightarrow\; \$\mathit{selves}_P[I].m() \]
For self-calls within a part's methods, the dispatch triple is \emph{(part, interface, method)} rather than the conventional
\emph{(object, method)}.
Figure~\ref{fig:dispatch-comparison} contrasts this per-interface routing with a single shared composite identity. This is
\emph{open recursion}: a method's self-calls late-bind to the most-derived receiver. Here the receiver resolves from the
interface of the self-call. Forwarding is the closed case: a component's self-calls stay in the component, and the composite
never enters the recursion.

\begin{invariant}
\label{prop:dispatch}
For any part $P$ in a delegation chain for interface $I$ rooted at composite $C$: a self-call \code{this.m()} within $P$
where $m \in I$ dispatches to $C$.
\end{invariant}

Composite identity is single-valued per interface. A part's \code{\$selves} slot for $I$ holds exactly one composite, established
at construction, with the most recent wiring of $I$ determining it. The root $C$ in the invariant denotes this current occupant
and is therefore always well-defined. Whether a given wiring order matches the programmer's intent is, as with any construction-time
assignment, the programmer's responsibility.

The invariant is established by construction. When composite $C$ establishes
a link to part $P$ for interface $I$, the plugin-generated
\code{\$linkPartToSelf} (Figure~\ref{fig:linkPartToSelf-listing}) sets $\$\mathit{selves}_P[I] \leftarrow C$ and
recurses into $P$'s own links, propagating $C$'s identity for each interface
$C$ claims and leaving all other slots unchanged. Each interface of a part resolves
through a single \code{\$selves} slot. Wiring an outer composite that claims
$I$ re-roots this slot to that composite and leaves the slots for interfaces it
does not claim unchanged, so the slot holds exactly one root at any time and
stabilizes once the outermost composite claiming $I$ is wired; $C$ denotes that
final root. Each write occurs during the wiring composite's construction. Wiring happens solely
as link field assignment, and the plugin enforces \code{final} on link
fields, so writes are confined to construction: the \code{\$selves} slots are never
written after the outermost composite claiming $I$ is constructed.

Visibility follows from safe publication. Once the composite root is safely published, the
Java Memory Model guarantees that any thread observing it sees all writes that
happened-before publication, the wired slots included. The role of \code{final}
here is write-confinement, not the JMM's final-field freeze. Since array-element writes
are not covered by freeze semantics, the guarantee rests on write-confinement
together with safe publication of the root, not on the finality of the slots.

Self-calls in $P$ where $m \in I$ are rewritten by the plugin to \code{((I)\,\$selves[IDX\_I]).m()}.
For self-calls through the delegation surface, there is no code path in a
part method that dispatches through anything other than the indexed
slot. Because the slot holds $C$ once the composition is constructed and is not
written thereafter, the invariant holds at every point a self-call can execute.
Before wiring, each slot is initialized to \code{this}; an unwired part
dispatches on itself, consistent with standard object-oriented dispatch. In this
initial state, the invariant holds trivially. A full mechanized proof in a Featherweight
Java subset is a natural direction for follow-on work (Section~\ref{sec:futurework}).

\subsection{Arbitrary Composition}
\label{subsec:topologies}

Mirroring the structural flexibility of standard object composition and forwarding, Parts supports unrestricted topologies.
These are expressed as behaviorally acyclic graphs where nodes are parts, composites, or ordinary objects, and edges are
link fields. Each edge establishes a connection from a composite to a component through the interface specified by the link.

Partial delegation permits a composite to claim only the interfaces it needs from a part's delegation surface, leaving the
remaining interfaces for other composites to claim. This freedom gives rise to the four composition topology primitives
shown in Figure~\ref{fig:topologies} and classified over two dimensions: the number of roots a part maintains and the number
of sources the composition has. A root is an outermost node claiming one or more interfaces of a part's delegation surface;
a source is a node with no incoming link edges.

\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] \textbf{Single-root, single-source.}
Composite $A$ implements $I_1, I_2$ and delegates both to $B$, which
implements both directly. $A$'s links claim all of $B$'s delegation surface,
so $B$ has one root, $A$, for both interfaces, and one source. This is the
topology typical of decorator chains.

\item[(b)] \textbf{Multi-root, single-source.}
Composite $A$ implements $I_1, I_2$ and delegates only $I_1$ to $B$,
which implements $I_1, I_2$ directly. Because $A$ does not claim $B$'s $I_2$,
$I_1$ is rooted in $A$, while $I_2$ is rooted in $B$: one source, two roots.
$A$ and $B$ in turn may delegate their free interfaces to separate components that
participate in another subgraph that likewise decomposes into one of
these four topologies. The motivating example discussed previously
(Figure~\ref{fig:dag-diagram}) is an instance of this topology combined with (a)
where $B$ delegates both its $I_1$ and $I_2$ to another part,
propagating $A$ and $B$ as the part's roots for $I_1$ and $I_2$.

\item[(c)] \textbf{Multi-root, multi-source.}
Component $C$ implements $I_1$ and $I_2$, where $A$ claims $I_1$ and
$B$ claims $I_2$. As in (b), $C$ has different roots for its two
interfaces, but here $A$ and $B$ are independent sources. This echoes
subject-oriented programming's independent \emph{subjects}, composed
with no single subject designated as owner (Section~\ref{sec:related}).
Figure~\ref{fig:topology-c-listing} exercises this directly.

\item[(d)] \textbf{Cyclic, multi-root, sourceless.}
Composite $A$ implements $I_1, I_2$ and delegates $I_1$ to $B$, which
implements $I_1, I_2$ and delegates $I_2$ back to $A$. Such structural cycles are
permitted when delegation through them is behaviorally acyclic
because each traversal proceeds through a distinct interface: $I_1$
is rooted in $A$, and $I_2$ is rooted in $B$. Cycles over nondistinct interfaces
are rejected (Section~\ref{sec:impl}).
\end{enumerate}

Each of the four topologies illustrated takes its reduced form using as few nodes and edges as possible while retaining
the essential properties that make the topology distinct. Topologies (a) and (b) each have a single source, but (b) is multi-root;
(c) is multi-root and multi-source; (d) is multi-root and sourceless. Topology (d) is structurally cyclic but behaviorally
acyclic over distinct interfaces.

These four are exhaustive over the two dimensions. Of the six combinations the dimensions admit, two cannot occur: a
single-rooted part forces a single source, and a sourceless graph is necessarily multi-root. This leaves (a)–(d).

Forwarding admits these topologies, so by definition a general delegation model must as well. The topologies therefore provide
a basis for verifying whether a delegation model supports arbitrary composition: does its treatment of open recursion remain
correct under arbitrary extensions of each topology?

To answer this question, another set of labeled edges may be drawn onto the structural delegation graph to form a second graph in which each part
is connected to the composites claiming its interfaces, determined by following the delegation edges backwards along each
interface of the part's delegation surface to the outermost composite claiming the interface. This derived identity graph
reveals what open recursion under arbitrary composition requires: composite identity assigned per interface.

In all four cases, for each delegated interface, a part acquires the identity of the composite claiming that interface,
independent of where execution enters the composition. Invariant~\ref{prop:dispatch} holds independently for each interface.

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:impl}
%% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The implementation is a javac plugin that operates directly on the compiler's AST before and after two phases: \emph{Enter}
(symbol definition) and \emph{Analyze} (semantic processing).

For the Enter phase, the plugin generates structural scaffolding. Here, \code{@part} classes are processed to add
the \code{\$selves} identity array, the \code{\$linkPartToSelf} wiring method, and if the class is abstract, the \code{\$Impl}
subclass and \code{asLink()} factory methods. Composite classes are also processed here to add forwarding methods corresponding
with \code{@link} delegation. At Analyze, \code{@part} classes are processed to rewrite self-calls and to enforce self-preservation
rules (Section~\ref{subsec:safety}). \code{@link} field assignment is also rewritten here to invoke wiring, and to enforce \code{@internal}.

\subsection{Part Transformation}

Each part holds a \code{\$selves} array with one slot for each interface on its delegation surface. The ordering of the
array's slots is compile-time-determined according to the part's class information.

\begin{lstlisting}[language={},style=java]
private final Object[] $selves = {this, ..., this};
// after wiring: $selves[IDX_I] = composite identity for interface I
\end{lstlisting}

Array slots are initialized with \code{this}, so a part's self-calls dispatch on itself by default.

Within a part, every call \code{this.m(args)} where \code{m} is declared in interface $I$ is rewritten to:

\begin{lstlisting}
((I) $selves[IDX_I]).m(args)
\end{lstlisting}

The cast ensures interface method invocation via the \code{invokeinterface} instruction, allowing the call to dispatch through
the composite's implementation of that interface.

The ordinal \code{IDX\_I} is assigned per part over its own delegation surface, so it is local to that class: the rewritten
self-calls and the generated \code{\$linkPartToSelf} both reside in the part and share its indexing, and no index agreement
across separately-compiled classes is required.

\begin{figure}[H]
\begin{lstlisting}
public void $linkPartToSelf(Object root, Class<?>[] linkScope) {
  // cycle detection
  if (this == root) Internal.reportCycle(this, root);
  // wire the part's 'linkScope' interfaces to 'root'
  outer:
  for(Class<?> linkIface : linkScope) {
    for(int i = 0; i < $INTERFACE_CLOSURE.length; ++i) {
      Class<?> iface = $INTERFACE_CLOSURE[i];
      if (iface == linkIface) {
        this.$selves[i] = root;
        continue outer;
      }
    }
    throw new DelegationLinkageError("Unimplemented linked interface: " + linkIface);
  }
  // recursively call $linkPartToSelf to rewire the part's related links to 'root'
  Class[] scopeLinkField1 = Internal.widest($LINK_FIELD_1, linkScope);
  if (scopeLinkField1.length != 0 && this.linkField1 instanceof $PartClass) {
    (($PartClass)this.linkField1).$linkPartToSelf(root, scopeLinkField1);
  }
  // likewise for the linkField2, etc.
}
\end{lstlisting}

ordered interface arrays, or an empty array if the arrays are not comparable.}
\label{fig:linkPartToSelf-listing}
\Description{\$linkPartToSelf code generation}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Composite Transformation}

A composite nominally implements every interface claimed by its links. For each method of a claimed interface that the composite
does not implement directly, the plugin generates a delegating method that forwards through the corresponding link field:

\begin{lstlisting}
R m(args) { return link.m(args); }
\end{lstlisting}

Each link field assignment \code{this.link = part} is transformed to invoke \code{part.\$linkPartToSelf(this, I.class)}, wiring the part's
\code{\$selves} slot for the link's declared interface $I$ to the composite. If the part itself links that interface to another part,
the generated method recursively rewires that linked part to the same composite (Figure~\ref{fig:linkPartToSelf-listing}).

\subsection{Abstract Part Support}

A part may be abstract, deferring some interface methods to the composite. Linking an abstract part obligates the composite
to implement those methods or to be declared abstract. When enforceable at compile time, the plugin generates a delegating
method for each interface method implemented by the part, but none for methods it leaves abstract. Without those methods, a
concrete composite is flagged by the compiler to either implement them directly or declare itself abstract. This is the
compositional analog of the rule that a concrete class must implement its inherited abstract methods.

To make an abstract part instantiable for linking, the compiler generates a private \code{\$Impl} inner subclass with stubs
that throw \code{DelegationLinkageError}, and an \code{asLink()} factory per constructor:

\begin{lstlisting}
private static final class $Impl extends AbstractClass {
  $Impl(/* AbstractClass ctor params */) { super(...); }
  @Override public R m(/* params */) {
    throw new DelegationLinkageError("Abstract method called");
  }
}
public static Foo asLink(/* ctor params */) { return new $Impl(...); }
\end{lstlisting}

Once wired, the stubs are unreachable for a statically configured abstract part. Self-calls dispatch through \code{\$selves}
to the composite, where the compile-time obligation guarantees an override. The stubs enforce the runtime configured case
when the linking composite does not override any of the part's abstract methods. The stubs also serve as a runtime guard
for direct abstract method calls on an \code{asLink()} instance.

\subsection{Default Methods}
\label{subsec:default}

If left unmitigated, a default method runs with the part as receiver, rather than the composite, when neither the composite
nor the part overrides it, resulting in incorrect self-call dispatch.

A default method cannot be directly invoked with a receiver other than the current \code{this}. The implementation uses a method handle
bound to an \code{invokedynamic} call site two work around this limitation. When a part does not override a default, the plugin
generates an override that invokes the interface's default implementation in this way, with the receiver taken from the
\code{\$selves} slot of the interface that \emph{provides} the default implementation, which may differ from the interface
that declares the method. The default body then runs with a \code{this} that implements the default's interface, so its
self-calls dispatch through the composite claiming that interface, as a self-call on that interface would.

\begin{lstlisting}
interface Foo {
  void bar();
  default void foo(P p) { bar(); } // self-call
}
@part class FooPart implements Foo {
  public void bar() { ... }
  // generated
  // calls Foo.super.foo() with composite in $selves as receiver
  public void foo(P p) {
    invokedynamic Foo::foo((Foo) $selves[IDX_Foo], p);
  }
}
\end{lstlisting}

The site's bootstrap links it once to a fixed target (the default implementation), with the receiver as its only varying
argument. Being monomorphic, it is inlined by the JIT to the same type of $O(1)$ array-indexed invocation as in Section~\ref{sec:eval}.

\subsection{Safety Guarantees}
\label{subsec:safety}

\paragraph{Cycle detection.}
If a part is wired to itself, directly or transitively, over the same interface, \code{De\-le\-ga\-tion\-Link\-age\-Er\-ror}
is thrown at assignment time during the recursive \code{\$linkPartToSelf()} call (Figure~\ref{fig:linkPartToSelf-listing}).

\paragraph{\code{@internal} enforcement.}
\code{@internal} may annotate an interface method or an interface type literal in a class's \code{implements} clause, with
distinct effects. A method annotated with \code{@internal} is accessible only within the composite, the same intent behind
\code{protected} in implementation inheritance, but more cleanly scoped for composition. A type literal in a class's \code{implements}
clause annotated with \code{@internal} removes that interface from the class's delegation surface: no composite may claim
it via a link field.

\paragraph{Ambiguous self-calls.}
A bare self-call in a part is ambiguous when its method is carried by two or more mutually unrelated interfaces (neither
assignable to the other), including when both inherit it from a common super-interface. The receiver must then be cast-qualified
as the intended interface (Figure~\ref{fig:overlapCall-listing}). Casting is otherwise optional. When a sub-interface and
its super-interface are claimed separately, for instance, ISD resolves a bare self-call through the super-interface, though
a part can still reach the sub-interface's override by casting to it. A cast-qualified \code{this} thus lets a part target
separate overrides of the same method, which the multi-rooted topologies~(b)--(d) (\S\ref{subsec:topologies}) require.

\paragraph{Self-preservation.}
Inside a part, the compiler restricts every use of \code{this}, implicit or explicit, that would expose the part's concrete
identity outside the composite dispatch path. \code{this} may appear only (1)~as a call or field-access receiver, where
interface-method calls route through ISD to the composite while every field access and non-interface call runs directly
on the part; (2)~as an interface-typed value in any other position, rewritten to the current occupant of that interface's
slot, either the claiming composite or, when the interface is unclaimed, the part itself; and (3)~as the operand of \code{synchronized},
which retains no reference. An escaping interface-typed \code{this} thus takes the same path as an interface self-call,
so a callee observes the same composition an external client would. Callbacks through the part's link fields, the compositional
analog of \code{super}, are likewise permitted. Every other occurrence, whether direct, inferred, or captured, is rejected,
including passing or assigning \code{this} as its concrete part type and casting it to \code{Object}.

A caller holding the bare part could invoke methods off the composite dispatch path and violate Invariant~\ref{prop:dispatch},
so the escape must be prevented, no legitimate use should require it. A part has no identity of its own beyond the composite, so
a reference to the raw component, rather than to an interface it satisfies, adds nothing the composite does not already
provide. Letting one escape could only split a single logical object in two, reintroducing the Object Schizophrenia
that ISD otherwise eliminates.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Dispatch Performance}

The dispatch path for a self-call through a composite is:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Load \code{\$selves[IDX\_I]}, an array-indexed load with compile-time
        constant index
  \item Cast to $I$
  \item \code{invokeinterface m}, standard JVM virtual dispatch
\end{enumerate}

This is $O(1)$. The parallel to vtable dispatch is direct. A vtable call is an
indexed slot load followed by an indirect call. A self-call within a part is
therefore on equal footing with a vtable call.

\paragraph{Benchmark.}
We verify this empirically using JMH~\cite{jmh} in average-time mode.
Each scenario calls \code{compute()} through a composite root at chain
depth $d$ (1--6). The \emph{baseline} traverses $d$ \code{@link} delegation
hops to a leaf that returns directly, with no self-call. The \emph{concrete}
variant traverses the same hops but the leaf issues a self-call
(\code{\$selves[IDX\_I].scale()}) from a concrete part method,
dispatching through the composite root. The \emph{default} variant issues the
same self-call from an inherited interface default method the leaf does not
override, routed through the generated \code{invokedynamic} override of
Section~\ref{subsec:default}. The delta (concrete minus baseline) isolates the cost of the self-call
dispatch mechanism alone. All runs: 10 JVM forks,
5 warmup iterations, 10 measurement iterations per fork (100 data points per
cell); errors are $\pm$\,half-width of the 99.9\% confidence interval.
A separate single-dispatch calibration run (\code{invokeinterface}
on a monomorphic call site, same harness) yields 0.43\,ns/op; the delta
values in Table~\ref{tab:bench} are therefore roughly 1--2$\times$ a single virtual
dispatch.

\paragraph{Choice of baseline.}
The delta is measured against Parts's own forwarding path, which establishes the
baseline: that path is semantically equivalent to Kotlin \code{by} and Lombok
\code{@Delegate} (Section~\ref{sec:related}), so the delta prices exactly the
self-call routing those mechanisms cannot express, against their own dispatch
cost. A cross-system benchmark against other composition models would price
differences in implementation strategy rather than the self-call dispatch this
delta isolates, so that comparison is analytic (Section~\ref{sec:related}).

\paragraph{Isolating the array index.}
The depth-series delta is essentially one virtual dispatch. To confirm the
\code{\$selves} array indexing adds nothing on top, we isolate the dispatch.
Holding a target method non-inlinable so a real call occurs, we compare three
depth-0 forms over the same receiver: a vtable call (\code{invokevirtual}), the
\code{invokeinterface} it compiles to, and the plugin's emitted form
\code{((I)\,\$selves[IDX\_I]).m()} (an \code{Object[]} element load, a cast, and
\code{invokeinterface}). Over 10 JVM forks (JDK~21) the three are statistically
indistinguishable. The ISD form differs from the bare \code{invokeinterface} it
emits by $0.03\pm0.09$\,ns, and from \code{invokevirtual} by $0.10\pm0.05$\,ns,
both within the fork-to-fork variance of the measurement. The array-element load
is not separately observable, therefore the self-call costs one virtual dispatch and no more.
This is the empirical content of the vtable-equivalence claim.

\paragraph{Construction cost.}
The result above is the steady-state call cost; wiring is paid once, at construction. Building
a composite invokes \code{part.\$linkPartToSelf} (Figure~\ref{fig:linkPartToSelf-listing}) at each link assignment involving a part, which
sets the part's relevant \code{\$selves} slots and recurses through the part's own link fields,
propagating composite identity for each interface the composite claims, where the interface is
comparable to the link field's interface. Each pass does constant work per part per claimed interface
(an identity check for cycle detection, a constant-time scope comparison, and no allocation).
Wrapping an already-linked composite re-propagates its subtree, so total wiring cost grows with
delegation depth, which is small in practice. None of this lies on the dispatch path, so the per-call
cost is unchanged.

\subsection{Example: Teaching Assistant}

The following example exercises diamond dispatch resolution and self-call routing through the composite. A teaching assistant (TA)
is both a student and a teacher where both roles share a common \code{Person} base.

\begin{lstlisting}
interface Person {
  String name();
  String title();
  String titledName();
}
interface Student extends Person { ... }
interface Teacher extends Person { ... }
interface TA extends Student, Teacher { ... }

@part abstract class PersonPart implements Person {
  private final String name;
  public PersonPart(String name) { this.name = name; }
  public String name() { return name; }
  public String titledName() { return title() + " " + name(); }
}

@part class StudentPart implements Student {
  @link Person person;
  public StudentPart(Person p) { this.person = p; }
  public String title() { return "Student"; }
}

@part class TeacherPart implements Teacher {
  @link Person person;
  public TeacherPart(Person p) { this.person = p; }
  public String title() { return "Teacher"; }
}

@part class TaPart implements TA {
  @link(share=Person.class) Student student;
  @link Teacher teacher;
  public TaPart(Student student) {
    this.student = student;
    this.teacher = new TeacherPart(student);
  }
  public String title() { return "TA"; }
}

TA ta = new TaPart(new StudentPart(PersonPart.asLink("Milton")));
ta.titledName(); // "TA Milton"
\end{lstlisting}

With forwarding, the shared \code{Person} methods
must each be resolved by hand, and \code{titledName()} still calls
\code{title()} on the delegate's own \code{this}. Parts resolves both problems.

\code{PersonPart} holds the template once. Its self-call to \code{title()}
dispatches through the composite at each level of the delegation chain.

\code{titledName()} is defined once in \code{PersonPart} and not overridden.
Diamond resolution is handled statically: \code{@link(share=Person.class)}
designates \code{student} as the forwarding link for \code{Person}, \code{TaPart}
transitively claims \code{Person} throughout the composite.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Partial delegation and part identity}
\label{subsec:identity}

\emph{Partial delegation} is a requirement for arbitrary composition, where a composite may claim only some of a part's
delegation surface. \emph{Multi-rooted parts} result from this condition: a part's interfaces may be rooted at two or more
distinct identities, including the part itself and one or more composites, via any combination of the multi-rooted topologies
in Section~\ref{subsec:topologies}.

The possession example in Section~\ref{sec:model} demonstrates an extended version of topology (b): \code{Demon} claims
only \code{Actor} from \code{Wizard}. Through delegation propagation, \code{Hero} consequently has separate roots for \code{Actor}
and \code{Combatant}, making it a multi-rooted part through a single source graph, \code{Demon}. The PaidSubscriber/SuperSoldierAddon
example (Figure~\ref{fig:topology-c-listing}) demonstrates topology (c): multiple roots within a multi-source composition.

This multi-rootedness makes a part's identity context-sensitive. References to \code{this}, whether implicit or explicit,
resolve according to the interface through which they are used. When that interface is claimed, ISD resolves \code{this}
to the claiming composite. Otherwise, \code{this} remains the part itself. This is a direct resolution of the Object Schizophrenia
problem mentioned in Section~\ref{sec:intro}.

\subsection{Equality}
Parts does not generate \code{equals} or \code{hashCode}. If object identity is insufficient for a composition's equality
or hashing checks and state must be taken into account, we suggest the conventional recipe for both part and non-part classes:
require that the argument's class is exactly the receiver's class (\code{getClass()}), compare the remaining owned state,
including link fields, and derive \code{hashCode} from the same state. This is the same recipe one would use for a decorator.

\subsection{Language Design Implications}

Parts addresses several longstanding limitations of implementation inheritance:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Fragile base class}~\cite{mikhajlov1998}: a composite's dependency surface is the interface contracts it
        explicitly claims, not a component's internal implementation; changes to its encapsulated internals do not propagate.
        Partial delegation completes the isolation: a composite is unaffected by changes to interfaces it does not claim.
  \item \textbf{Hierarchy-bound}: implementation inheritance binds behavior to a class hierarchy and a single object identity,
        making independently rooted compositions of the same component impossible. Parts permits arbitrary topologies by
        assigning composite identity per interface.
  \item \textbf{The \emph{Object Schizophrenia} problem~\cite{sekharaiah2002}}: a hazard with forwarding
        where self-calls remain within the component rather than routing through the composite; resolved by ISD.
  \item \textbf{Diamond ambiguity}: Parts provides explicit \code{@link(share=I.class)} resolution; ambiguous diamonds left
        unresolved are compile errors rather than being silently merged.
  \item \textbf{Unintended interface exposure}: a composite may implement a subset of its parts' interfaces; those it does
        not implement are absent from its API by construction. \code{@internal} strengthens this further: a class may declare selected
        interfaces as internal to its implementation and excluded from its delegation surface, preventing the
        interfaces from being claimed by a composite.
  \item \textbf{Abstract behavior}: abstract parts with deferred linkage (\code{asLink()}) preserve the template-method
        capability of abstract classes without coupling their implementation and hierarchy.
\end{itemize}

Implementation inheritance's power is open recursion. Its fragility is the surface that power ranges over---every accessible
member. Parts retains open recursion but bounds the surface to the declared interface contract. Self-calls still reach the
composite's overrides, while the internals that make base classes fragile are never exposed.

Implementation inheritance and Parts are, essentially, two bindings of the same open recursion: implementation inheritance
binds it to the class hierarchy, where a subclass IS-A its superclass and inherits its entire implementation surface, while
Parts binds it to a held object and its delegation surface, where the composite HAS-A its parts and IS-A only its interfaces.
The concept that inheritance and open recursion are separable is Cook's~\cite{cookpalsberg1989,cook1990}, and the modeling
of inheritance as composition is Bracha and Cook's~\cite{bracha1990}. Cook's model threads self as an explicit parameter;
Parts wires composite identity per-interface at construction.

\subsection{Concurrency}

A constructed composition is safe to share across threads. Each interface's \code{\$selves} slot may be written during construction
as links are established; the last write determines the resulting root. Once construction is complete, the slots are never
written again, so the JMM makes the final wired state visible to any thread observing the safely published root. No synchronization
is required on the dispatch path. The guarantee is scoped to a single composition and relies on the single-value identity
per interface established in Section~\ref{sec:model}. Parts may hold mutable state under standard Java rules and dispatch
adds no synchronization points.

\subsection{Future Work}
\label{sec:futurework}

\paragraph{Per-interface composite roots as a general primitive.}
Assigning dispatch identity per interface at construction is, perhaps, a primitive that may be applied in areas beyond this
paper's focus. It is an unexplored subject whether problems currently addressed through dynamic dispatch, AOP weaving, or
collapsed component hierarchies could benefit from the same mechanism.

\paragraph{Formal verification.}
A mechanized proof of Invariant~\ref{prop:dispatch} using small-step operational semantics would provide rigorous structural
verification beyond the constructive proof sketch outlined in Section~\ref{sec:model}. Because the Parts model maps cleanly
onto a core calculus, a modified subset of Featherweight Java serves as a natural starting point for formalizing these type-safety
and execution guarantees.

\paragraph{Link timing and mutability.}
Through \code{final} links, composite identity is finalized at construction. This read-only guarantee is what makes sharing
lock-free (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}): stable wiring needs no synchronization on the dispatch path. Two relaxations of
the rule, opt-in \emph{lazy} and \emph{mutable} links, are worth exploring, and they go in opposite directions.

A \emph{lazy} link changes only the timing. Backed by the stable-value facility of recent
JDK releases (Project Leyden), it wires on first use instead of at construction, yet stays written once and
effectively final, so the guarantee holds while link initialization is deferred. A \emph{mutable} link
lifts the restriction. Mutating link assignments become legal, restoring the only aspect
of conventional forwarding Parts disallows (runtime reconfiguration) but sacrifices the
guarantee and under the same thread-safety obligations as a regular mutable field.
Both relax restrictions of the current model rather than changing the underlying composition model.
Whether a mutable link can also keep the guarantee, by confining rewiring to safe-publication points, is an open question.

\paragraph{Domain modeling and persistence integration.}
Normalized relational schemas naturally represent domain types as independently owned sets of data and relationships, whereas
object-relational mapping often pressures those types into inheritance hierarchies. Table-per-hierarchy collapses types
into a single hierarchy, often producing sparse, null-heavy tables; table-per-subclass preserves a more normalized representation
but retains the coupling of inheritance. Conventional composition avoids that coupling but gives up open recursion. Parts
is positioned to remove this tradeoff: independently modeled types can remain separate components, composed according to the domain's relationships
while retaining open recursion across the composition. This suggests a domain model in which the object structure can mirror
the normalized schema directly rather than introducing an inheritance hierarchy solely to retain open recursion. How such
a mapping could be realized as a persistence framework is an open question.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
%% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

While Parts assumes static typing, this section also considers dynamically typed
models where the structural comparisons remain conceptually instructive. The
discussion is organized around the three properties that characterize Parts:
independent runtime components, arbitrary composition, and open recursion.

\paragraph{Forwarding and conventional composition.}
Kotlin's \code{by} keyword~\cite{kotlin}, Lombok's \code{@Delegate}~\cite{lombok},
Scala~3's \code{export}~\cite{scala3export}, and Go's struct
embedding~\cite{go} provide forwarding from a composite to a supplied component.
These mechanisms preserve independent runtime objects and permit the component
structure to be composed freely. Self-calls, however, remain in the component's
scope rather than dispatching through the composite. They therefore provide the
first two properties but not open recursion.

\paragraph{Mixins and traits.}
Bracha and Cook~\cite{bracha1990} formalized mixins as parameterized abstract
subclasses, while Sch\"arli et al.~\cite{scharli2003} introduced traits as
stateless, composable units of behavior. Odersky and Zenger~\cite{odersky2005}
later introduced statically typed mixin composition with abstract type members.
Scala's \code{with} clause linearizes traits into the target class at compile
time, so self-calls dispatch through the outermost class in the resulting
hierarchy. These systems therefore provide open recursion, but composition
occurs by fusing the contributed behavior into a single class or object rather
than by supplying an independent component at construction. A component
implementation cannot consequently be selected or replaced independently at
construction, nor can a single component instance participate independently in
multiple composites. This fusion is also characteristic of recent
precisely-typed mixin systems~\cite{fanparreaux2023}. Parts separates the two:
open recursion is provided by interface-scoped dispatch without fusing the
component into the composite.

Chugh's ISOLATE~\cite{chugh2015} gives a statically typed calculus for
functional objects in which \emph{premethods} are defined apart from a host
object and later mixed into it. ISOLATE provides typed open recursion without
an explicit fixpoint, but the resulting object is still assembled by mixin
composition rather than from an independently supplied runtime component.
It therefore occupies the same side of the tradeoff: open recursion without
independent runtime components.

\paragraph{Family polymorphism and dynamic composition.}
Family-oriented systems such as gbeta~\cite{ernst2001,ernst2006}, CaesarJ~\cite{aracic2006},
Object Teams/Java~\cite{herrmann2002}, and J\&~\cite{nystrom2006}
support extensible families whose members can participate in open recursive
composition. Their object structure, however, is defined by the family model:
members are instantiated as part of the family rather than supplied as
independent runtime components.

Newspeak~\cite{bracha2010} makes names late-bound and permits classes to be
supplied as first-class constructor arguments, supporting runtime wiring and
reconfiguration of module objects. Its open recursion derives from mixin
inheritance~\cite{bracha1990}: classes are applied to superclasses and fused
into a single object. Composed module objects are instead held by reference
and reached through ordinary message sends, so self-calls do not route through
the composition. Newspeak therefore combines dynamic composition with open
recursive inheritance, but does not provide open recursion across independently
composed runtime objects in the Parts sense.

\paragraph{Delegation.}
Several research systems have explored delegation as a way to retain independent
runtime objects while restoring open recursion.

Kniesel's DARWIN~\cite{kniesel1999,kniesel2000} provides statically typed
delegation-based single implementation inheritance. A base object is supplied
at run time through a \code{delegatee} field, and self-calls route through the
inheriting subtype. DARWIN permits at most one delegating or forwarding field
per class~\cite[\S6.5]{kniesel2000}; multiple delegation is deliberately
excluded~\cite[\S6.5.1]{kniesel2000}. A receiver typed through a single
interface therefore cannot represent multiple independently rooted
compositions~\cite[\S5.5.4]{kniesel2000}. DARWIN provides independent
components and open recursion, but is effectively limited to the
single-root, single-source topology (a).

Incomplete Featherweight Java (IFJ)~\cite{bettini2011} similarly provides
static typing, independent runtime objects, and open recursion through
\emph{incomplete objects} completed by other objects at run time. Its
delegation is structural and per method, however, with a single \code{this}
binding for the delegation chain. A component therefore cannot root different
interfaces at different composites. Its composition is consequently confined
to topology (a)~(\S\ref{subsec:topologies}). Parts instead associates each
interface with a specific composite through \code{@link}, permitting the
multi-rooted and multi-source topologies induced by partial delegation.

DelphJ~\cite{gerakios2013} is a Java-based research language that augments its MorphJ metaprogramming DSL with delegation
features (\emph{access-paths}) as a means for open recursion. Its runtime-supplied \emph{subobjects} generally support late-bound
self-calls, but the access-path mechanism is designed around a single-rooted delegation structure, which incurs substantial
runtime costs from constructing and merging access paths for stack-held references and traversing them on self-calls. Crucially, the
access-path strategy does not provide the per-interface identity required by arbitrary composition. In particular, the model
cannot correctly resolve cross-interface self-calls to methods common to separately claimed interfaces, as required by topologies
(b),(d) (Figure~\ref{fig:overlapCall-listing}). Additionally, access paths cannot support cross-interface self-calls at all
with topology~(c) (\S\ref{subsec:topologies}). Its composition is therefore limited to topology~(a).

Generally, delegation models such as these that emulate inheritance's tree-shaped hierarchies using caller-side dispatch
mechanisms are bound to topology~(a) because multi-rooted topologies ultimately require separate identities for each delegated
interface. Parts inverts the caller-side assumption: composite identity is fixed in the component rather than reconstructed
by the caller on every access. This single design choice is what makes delegation under arbitrary composition possible.

\paragraph{Delegation at larger granularity.}
Ostermann's delegation layers~\cite{ostermann2002} extend delegation from
individual objects to entire collaborations composed at run time. Operations
are routed to the composed collaboration rather than to a single layer,
providing ``composition consistency.'' The unit of composition is a
collaboration, however, whose participants are nested classes redirected
through the enclosing instance. The model therefore provides open recursion
at collaboration granularity rather than through independently supplied
components, and does not provide the per-component partial delegation that
gives rise to multi-rooted and multi-source topologies.

Prototype-based delegation provides another form of open recursion.
Lieberman~\cite{lieberman1986} introduced \emph{open delegation}, in which
an object delegates unanswered messages to a prototype while \code{self}
remains the original receiver; Self~\cite{self1987} realized this model in a
working language. These systems support late-bound components and open
recursion, but delegation is object-granular rather than interface-scoped.
A prototype supplies all behavior not handled by the delegating object, so
partial delegation and the resulting multi-rooted topologies are not
expressible. Their dynamic typing also provides no static interface contract
for the composed result. Cecil~\cite{chambers1993} explores a statically typed
point in this space, separating subtyping from code inheritance, but does not
provide dynamic inheritance of the kind relevant here.

Subject-oriented programming~\cite{harrison1993,ossher1995} is particularly
relevant to multi-source composition. It composes independent
\emph{subjects}, each representing a complete view of a shared domain, using
externally specified composition rules such as \code{merge} and
\code{override}. The model explicitly targets decentralized composition in
which no subject is the sole owner of the result. This corresponds closely to
the multi-source structure of topology (c). The original model and its
implementations, however, do not preserve independently composed runtime
objects in the final representation and do not provide static typing across
the composed result~\cite{ossher1995}. Parts addresses the same structural
problem at the granularity of an individual runtime object, using
interface-scoped identity under a statically checked interface contract.

\paragraph{Multiple inheritance.}
C++ virtual base classes and virtual inheritance~\cite{stroustrup1987}
resolve inheritance diamonds within a single object and its merged class
hierarchy. They therefore address ambiguity among inherited implementations
rather than composition among independently supplied runtime components.
Multiple inheritance does not provide the independent component identity or
dynamic composition considered by Parts.

\paragraph{Novelty.}
The surveyed systems divide naturally according to the three properties of
Parts. Forwarding provides independent runtime components and arbitrary
composition, but not open recursion. Mixins, traits, family systems, and
related forms of class composition provide open recursion, but fuse the
components into a statically determined object structure. Delegation systems
such as DARWIN, IFJ, and DelphJ restore open recursion while retaining
independent runtime components, but restrict the composition topologies they
can represent.

The closest prior systems are therefore the delegation models. DARWIN
deliberately excludes multiple delegation~\cite[\S6.5.1]{kniesel2000}; IFJ
binds a single \code{this} across its delegation structure; and DelphJ's
access-path mechanism, the most capable of the three, still limits composition
to only the simplest single-rooted case. Of the three properties, arbitrary
composition is the practically unobtainable one under caller-side delegation models.

Parts unifies the three properties through interface-scoped dispatch.
By assigning composite identity per interface at construction, ISD allows a
single component to participate in the arbitrary compositions of forwarding while
preserving late-bound self-calls. The resulting self-dispatch path has
$O(1)$ cost and is empirically equivalent to a standard vtable call.

%% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}
%% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The familiar advice to favor object composition over class inheritance leaves an important tradeoff unresolved: composition
gives up the open recursion that makes inheritance so effective for extension. Parts resolves the tradeoff. By assigning
composite identity per interface at construction, it shows that open recursion need not depend on inheritance's single runtime
object, and that the two can be decoupled in practice. The result is a general model that retains the essential properties
of simple forwarding, namely independent runtime components and arbitrary composition topologies, while adding open recursion
at vtable speed.
